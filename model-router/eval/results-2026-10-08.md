# Routing sub-agent tasks with Jev: three decision models

**2026-10-08 · 24 tasks · TypeSafe Jev (hosted), Open Jev 27B and Open Jev Flash 9B (local) ·
sources: `results-2026-10-08T16-41-27-867Z.json` (27B), `results-2026-10-08T18-41-32-335Z.json`
(Flash 9B), `results-2026-10-08T18-51-11-416Z.json` (TypeSafe Jev)**

An agent harness has to pick a model each time its orchestrator spawns a sub-agent. model-router
makes that choice with one request to a System One model (Jev), which returns probabilities, not
text. This is the first live run: 24 hand-written tasks, each sent once to three decision models.
One is TypeSafe's hosted Jev. The other two are [Open Jev](https://huggingface.co/openjev) models
running locally: the 27B model, and Flash 9B, a smaller one released the day before.

## Result

| Decision model | Routed as labelled | Routed too low | Routed too high | Kept off the top model | Median time per decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TypeSafe Jev (hosted) | 24 of 24 (100%) | 0 | 0 | 16 of 24 (67%) | 93 ms |
| Open Jev Flash 9B (local) | 24 of 24 (100%) | 0 | 0 | 16 of 24 (67%) | 181 ms |
| Open Jev 27B (local) | 23 of 24 (96%) | 0 | 1 | 16 of 24 (67%) | 567 ms |

- **No model sent a task too low.** The only miss, by the 27B, went one tier too high.
- **TypeSafe Jev and the 27B were sure of their answers.** Each put 0.95 or more on one route for
  20 of the 24 tasks. Flash 9B did for 5.
- **Flash 9B's 24 of 24 depends on the routing rule** described below. Taking its likeliest route
  would have sent 3 tasks to too weak a model. For the other two models the rule changed nothing.
- **The hosted model was fastest**, network round trip included. Of the two local models, Flash 9B
  was about three times faster than the 27B, at about a third of the download (5 GB against
  14 GB).

The sample is small and the tasks are easy to tell apart, so read this as "the mechanism works",
not as a measured accuracy. The caveats are at the end.

## What Jev was asked

Each request carries the task text and one Score question. A Score places the input on an ordered
list of levels and returns a probability for each. The three levels are descriptions of kinds of
work, cheapest first. Each one maps to a model in the router's config, and Jev never sees the
model names.

> **Which description fits the work that `task` asks a sub-agent to do?**
>
> 0. Finding, reading, listing or summarising what already exists, or making one small change
>    whose exact content is given. Every step is known before starting.
> 1. Writing or changing code or text to a clear specification within a few files: a described
>    feature, a bug with a known cause, tests for existing behaviour. The goal is settled, and how
>    to reach it takes some judgment.
> 2. Work where the approach itself has to be worked out: a failure with an unknown cause, a
>    design or plan with trade-offs, a change that reaches across many parts of a system, or a
>    review that has to find subtle faults.

| Level | Route | Model the harness would run |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | `lookup` | `claude-haiku-5-5` |
| 1 | `standard` | `claude-sonnet-5-5` |
| 2 | `open_ended` | `claude-opus-5-5` |

## How an answer becomes a route

Jev's answer is three probabilities that add up to 1: how likely each description is the one that
fits the task. For task 5 ("Run the test suite and report which tests fail, with the first line of
each error. Do not fix anything.") the 27B model's answer was:

| lookup | standard | open_ended |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.84 | 0.08 | 0.08 |

The obvious policy is to take the likeliest route, `lookup`. The router asks something different
of each route: **if the task is sent here, how likely is it that it needed a more capable
model?** That is the sum of the probabilities of every route above it.

| If the task is sent to | It needed more if the right route was | Chance of that |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `lookup` | `standard` or `open_ended` | 0.08 + 0.08 = **0.16** |
| `standard` | `open_ended` | **0.08** |
| `open_ended` | nothing is above it | **0** |

The router takes the cheapest route whose chance is at or under a limit, called the bar:

| Bar | `lookup` (0.16) | `standard` (0.08) | Task 5 goes to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.25 (default) | passes | | `lookup` |
| 0.10 | fails | passes | `standard` |
| 0.05 | fails | fails | `open_ended` |

The most capable route always passes, since nothing is above it, so there is always a route to
take.

Task 5 is the unusual case for the 27B. Most of its answers looked like task 9 ("Add a --dry-run flag to the sync
command..."), which was 0.00 / 0.99 / 0.01. Sent to `lookup`, the chance it needed more is
0.99 + 0.01 = 1.00, which fails every bar. Sent to `standard`, the chance is 0.01, which passes
every bar. It goes to `standard` at 0.25, at 0.10 and at 0.05: when Jev is sure, the bar makes no
difference.

### Why not just take the biggest number?

Because an unsure answer should go to a stronger model, not to whichever route edges ahead.

Flash 9B's answer for task 15 ("Add a retry with exponential backoff, at most three attempts, to
the webhook sender when the response is a 5xx") was 0.54 / 0.43 / 0.04. The biggest number is
`lookup`, at 0.54. But there is a 0.46 chance the task needs more than `lookup`, which is over the
bar of 0.25, so this method sends it to `standard`, the route it was labelled with.

That happened on 3 of the 24 tasks with Flash 9B (14, 15 and 16). Taking the biggest number would
have sent all three to the weakest model.

Sending a task too low means a failed run and a retry. Sending it too high only costs more. When
Jev is sure, as in task 9, both methods pick the same route.

## Where the models differed

They chose a different route for one task, and ranked the routes differently on three more.

- **Task 7** was the only miss. A rename of a constant that is "defined and used twice" was
  labelled `lookup`, and the 27B sent it to `standard` (0.17 / 0.83 / 0.00). The `lookup`
  description says "one small change whose exact content is given", and a rename across a
  definition and two uses is three edits, which may be why. The other two models sent it to
  `lookup`: TypeSafe Jev at 0.98 and Flash 9B at 0.79.
- **Tasks 14, 15 and 16** are small, well-specified changes labelled `standard`. Flash 9B split
  all three between `lookup` and `standard`, with `lookup` slightly ahead each time (0.54), and
  the rule moved them up to `standard`. The 27B was sure of all three (0.95, 0.99 and 0.95 for
  `standard`). TypeSafe Jev was sure of 14 and 15 (0.94 and 0.99) and less sure of 16, the README
  task (0.63).

Three things to note:

- **The bar barely matters for TypeSafe Jev and the 27B.** At 0.25 the rule moved nothing for
  either: every task went to its likeliest route, because every answer was confident enough.
  TypeSafe Jev routes all 24 as labelled at any bar from 0.10 up.
- **It matters for Flash 9B.** At 0.25 the rule moved three tasks up, each to its labelled route.
  At 0.50 those same three go too low, the only tasks routed too low anywhere in these runs. At
  0.05 five tasks go too high.
- **A move isn't always one tier.** On task 3 ("list the environment variables this service
  reads") TypeSafe Jev and the 27B both put nearly all their doubt on `open_ended`, not on
  `standard`. TypeSafe Jev's answer was 0.90 / 0.02 / 0.08, so at a bar of 0.05 the task goes
  from the cheapest route straight to the most expensive.

## What this does and doesn't show

It shows that the request works end to end, that a Score over route descriptions separates clear
cases, that a model a third of the size can do the job in a third of the time when the routing
rule allows for its doubt, and that the policy can be tuned after the fact without asking again.

It doesn't show how well the router would do in a real harness:

- **24 tasks, asked once each.** One task is 4 percentage points.
- **One author wrote everything.** Claude wrote the tasks, their labels and the three route
  descriptions in one sitting, so the tasks echo the wording they are judged against ("find the
  cause" against "a failure with an unknown cause"). Real sub-agent prompts are longer, messier
  and less obviously one kind of work.
- **Labels are opinions.** Nobody ran these tasks on the three Claude models to see which was enough.
  The check that matters is whether tasks sent to a cheaper model succeed as often as on the
  most expensive one, and that needs a harness.
- **Flash 9B's 24 of 24 has little margin.** On four tasks (13 to 16) it was close to an even
  split between two routes. A different bar, or slightly different wording, could change the
  result.
- **The tasks don't separate the models.** All three are at or near 24 of 24, so this set says
  nothing about which decides best, only that all three can do it and how sure each is.

To reproduce, run the three evals one after another. Each sends its 24 requests one at a time.
The ports are only how the router tells the two local servers apart (27B on 3002, Flash 9B on
3003). For the numbers above, only one local server was running at a time.

```bash
cd model-router
TYPESAFE_API_KEY=sk-... TYPESAFE_DEFAULT_MODEL=jev-1.13.0 node eval/run.mjs
JEV_BACKEND=openjev node eval/run.mjs
JEV_BACKEND=openjev OPENJEV_URL=http://127.0.0.1:3003 node eval/run.mjs
node eval/compare.mjs eval/results-*.json
```
