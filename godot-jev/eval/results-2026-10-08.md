# Parsing player slang with Jev: three decision models

**2026-10-08 · 16 lines × 3 rounds · TypeSafe Jev (hosted), Open Jev 27B and Open Jev Flash 9B
(local) · source: `results-2026-10-08T134205.json`**

*The Sunken Crypt* sends whatever the player typed to a System One model (Jev), with the moves
possible right now as the options of one Choice. Jev returns probabilities, not text, and the
game's parser decides from the confidence whether to act, to ask "Did you mean:", or to say the
request isn't possible. This run gives the same 16 lines to three decision models in the same game
states. One is TypeSafe's hosted Jev. The other two are [Open Jev](https://huggingface.co/openjev)
models running locally: the 27B model, and Flash 9B, which is new since the two-model run of
2026-09-30.

The interactive version of this report is on the
[docs site](https://alteredcraft.github.io/system-one-experiments/godot-jev/).

## Result

At the game's thresholds: act at a confidence of 0.75 or more, ask from 0.40.

| Decision model | Lines landed | Acted at once | Asked "did you mean?" | Unknown | Wrong moves | Median time per turn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TypeSafe Jev (hosted) | 48 of 48 | 45 | 0 | 3 | 0 | 165 ms |
| Open Jev Flash 9B (local) | 48 of 48 | 39 | 6 | 3 | 0 | 185 ms |
| Open Jev 27B (local) | 48 of 48 | 45 | 0 | 3 | 0 | 579 ms |

A line lands when the game acts on the move it means, or asks and offers that move. The gin line
means nothing the author wrote, and lands when it comes back unknown: that is the 3 in each row.

- **No wrong move in 144 turns.** Every model ranked the move the line means first on every turn,
  and every pair of models ranked the same option first on all 48.
- **They differ in certainty.** Turns with a confidence of 0.95 or more: the 27B 45, TypeSafe Jev
  34, Flash 9B 9, out of 48. Mean confidence: 0.98, 0.95 and 0.83.
- **Less certainty shows up as questions.** Flash 9B was under the act threshold on two lines
  ("cop that shortsword, fo shizzle" at 0.48 and "what that lil paper say, homie?" at 0.74), so the
  game asked, with the right move as option 1. The other two models acted on both.
- **The times are readings of this setup, not of the models.** See Latency below before quoting
  them.

The sample is 16 lines in one voice, so read this as "the mechanism works on three models", not as
a measured accuracy. The caveats are at the end.

## What Jev was asked

Each request carries a small state, the player's words, and one Choice. For line 9, typed in the
hall of pillars with the torch and the sword in hand:

```json
{
  "carrying": ["torch", "notched shortsword"],
  "dark": false,
  "location": "Hall of pillars",
  "player_typed": "lay the smack down on Mr. Bones wit the blade",
  "visible": []
}
```

> **Which action is the player asking to take?**
>
> `look__around` look around the room · `inventory__self` check what I'm carrying ·
> `go__south` · `go__east` · `go__west` · `go__north` · `examine__torch` · `examine__sword` ·
> `drop__torch` · `drop__sword` · `attack__skeleton` attack the skeleton with the shortsword ·
> `none_of_these` The player isn't asking for any of these: a question, small talk, or something
> not possible here.

The options are rebuilt every turn from the world's rules, so the list runs from 6 to 13 options
along the route. The model can only answer with one of them.

## How an answer becomes a turn

Jev returns a probability for every option and a confidence in the one it chose. The parser
(`demo/parser.gd`) reads the confidence:

| Confidence | The game | What the player sees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≥ 0.75 | acts | the move's authored response |
| ≥ 0.40 | clarifies | "Did you mean:" and the two likeliest moves |
| lower, or the choice is "none of these" | unknown | an authored line such as "Nothing here answers to that." |

A wrong move changes the game's state. A question costs the player one keypress. Flash 9B's answer
to line 6 put 0.54 on taking the shortsword, 0.13 on examining it and 0.12 on taking the vellum,
with a confidence of 0.48: the game asked "Did you mean: 1. take the notched shortsword 2. examine
the notched shortsword".

## Where the models differed

- **Lines 6 and 7** are the only ones where the game did something different. Flash 9B ranked the
  right move first on both, under the act threshold. Line 7 missed narrowly: at an act threshold
  of 0.74 it acts.
- **Line 1**, the gin line, came back unknown from all three, because each chose "none of these".
  TypeSafe Jev put 0.97 on it and the 27B 0.98. Flash 9B put 0.60, with 0.10 on checking the
  inventory and 0.09 on examining the torch. The parser treats a choice of "none" as unknown at any
  confidence, so the thresholds played no part. One line is too few to say how often Flash 9B
  would pick a move here.

## Moving the thresholds

The same answers, replayed through the parser at other thresholds. No new requests are made. Of
the 45 turns per model that mean a move:

| Act at | Ask at | TypeSafe Jev: act / ask / dead end | Flash 9B: act / ask / dead end | 27B: act / ask / dead end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.60 | 0.40 | 45 / 0 / 0 | 42 / 3 / 0 | 45 / 0 / 0 |
| **0.75** | **0.40** (the game) | 45 / 0 / 0 | 39 / 6 / 0 | 45 / 0 / 0 |
| 0.85 | 0.40 | 43 / 2 / 0 | 27 / 18 / 0 | 45 / 0 / 0 |
| 0.90 | 0.40 | 35 / 10 / 0 | 18 / 27 / 0 | 45 / 0 / 0 |
| 0.95 | 0.40 | 31 / 14 / 0 | 9 / 36 / 0 | 42 / 3 / 0 |
| 0.75 | 0.50 | 45 / 0 / 0 | 39 / 3 / 3 | 45 / 0 / 0 |
| 0.75 | 0.75 | 45 / 0 / 0 | 39 / 0 / 6 | 45 / 0 / 0 |

- **No setting produces a wrong move in this run**, because every model ranked the right option
  first. Here the thresholds only trade acting against asking, and asking against a dead end. They
  protect against a confident wrong choice, and these lines never produced one.
- **The act threshold matters most for the least sure model.** TypeSafe Jev acts on every move up
  to a threshold of 0.81, the 27B up to 0.93, Flash 9B only up to 0.48.
- **Raising the ask threshold turns questions into dead ends.** At 0.50, Flash 9B's line 6 comes
  back unknown in every round, and the player is told the request isn't possible when it is.

## Latency

**These numbers are not a statement of how fast the models are.** The two Open Jev figures are
what one laptop measured: 4-bit MLX builds on an Apple M5 Pro running macOS, a desktop operating
system and not a server. Both local models were loaded at the same time, and an editor, a browser
and other applications were running. The same weights on server hardware, at another quantisation
or under another runtime would time differently. The TypeSafe figure is a round trip over the
network from that laptop to a hosted API, so it mixes their serving time with our connection.

| Decision model | min | p50 | mean | p95 | max | Check 2 (p95 ≤ 500 ms) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TypeSafe Jev (hosted) | 138 ms | 165 ms | 168 ms | 200 ms | 213 ms | pass |
| Open Jev Flash 9B (local) | 143 ms | 185 ms | 176 ms | 200 ms | 228 ms | pass |
| Open Jev 27B (local) | 434 ms | 579 ms | 546 ms | 620 ms | 634 ms | misses by 120 ms |

Measured in the game loop around each parse, one request at a time, after one untimed warm-up per
model. The headless loop ticks about every 7 ms, so times fall on steps of that size.

The local models took longer as the request grew along the route, with more options and more items
in the state: the 27B from about 448 ms on line 1 to about 618 ms on line 16, Flash 9B from about
149 ms to about 200 ms. The hosted model showed no such trend at this size.

The same lines on 2026-09-30, with the same pinned TypeSafe model and the same 27B build, gave a
hosted p50 of 213 ms and p95 of 502 ms (two slow round trips, the slowest 1,144 ms), and a 27B p50
of 600 ms and p95 of 683 ms. The model versions were the same on both days and the conditions were
not.

## What this does and doesn't show

It shows that the same game, parser and thresholds work unchanged on all three decision models,
that a Choice over the possible moves maps heavy slang to the right authored move, that a less
sure model produces questions and not wrong moves, and that the thresholds can be tried after the
fact without asking again.

It doesn't show how well the parser would do with real players:

- **16 lines, in one voice.** They were written to solve the dungeon in order for a recording, not
  sampled from what players type. One line is 6 percentage points.
- **Three rounds are not three samples.** The two local models gave the same answer to a line
  every round, so Flash 9B's 6 questions are 2 lines asked 3 times.
- **One impossible request.** Check 1 needs many: wrong-room requests, items that aren't there,
  small talk.
- **No typos, no indirect requests**, and nothing ambiguous between two moves in the same room.
- **Short option lists.** Between 6 and 13 options a turn, against the 255 a Choice can take.
- **The lines don't separate the models.** All three landed 48 of 48, so this says nothing about
  which understands best, only that all three can do it and how sure each is.

This is not evidence for check 1, which needs the corpus in the README's first Open item.
